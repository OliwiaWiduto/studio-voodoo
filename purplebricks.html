Bringing a 12-year-old experience back to life

The Purplebricks search and listings experience was designed in 2014. It hadn’t been touched since.

Twelve years later, it was still one of the highest-traffic areas of the product. As part of our mission to become the Monzo of property, it was an obvious place to start. The challenge wasn’t just to make it look cleaner. We wanted to help people find the right property faster, while stripping back the brand and putting the focus where it belonged: on the customer.

At the start of the project, the pages attracted around 1,456 monthly visitors. After the redesign, that grew to an average of 2,345.

Here’s how we got there.

Challenging the status quo

The CEO started the project by creating his own design to map out his vision for the new experience.

That created an interesting challenge.

The existing product had been shaped directly by him, so introducing a more considered design approach meant I couldn’t simply come in and say, “this needs to change.” I needed to understand the thinking behind the design, then use design principles, consistency and customer needs to show where we could make it stronger.

A few of the biggest changes came from questioning some of the assumptions baked into the old experience.

Search results that actually feel like results

In the old experience, listings appeared below the fold. Users could perform a search and still not immediately see the results.

We moved the results onto a dedicated page, making the interaction feel much clearer and giving users immediate feedback that their search had worked.

Lead with what buyers care about

The old listings led with the property title.

But a property name isn't what helps you decide whether you want to see a house. The two things buyers judge first are how it looks and how much it costs.

So we changed the hierarchy to lead with price and imagery, putting the information that mattered most front and centre.

Give the property room to sell itself

The original listings used relatively small images.

We gave photography much more space with a large image banner and introduced a pop-out gallery so users could explore the property without losing their place.

The principle was simple: if you're buying a home, you want to see it.

The design system problem

While all of this was happening, we were also introducing a new design system.

This created another challenge: the CEO had been burned by previous design systems that felt restrictive and slowed the team down.

So I didn't want to sell him a library of components: I wanted to sell him a better way of building.

In our alignment session, I framed the design system as a set of Lego bricks for his vision. Instead of repeatedly recreating his ideas from scratch, we could document them as flexible components that the whole team could use.

That meant:

  1. Designers could work within the same visual language.
  2. The product could stay consistent as it grew.
  3. New designers could understand the rules without needing everything explained to them.
  4. We could spend less time recreating UI and more time solving actual customer problems.

For a team of three designers, that gave us a much stronger foundation to scale the design function and increase delivery speed.

More than a facelift

The result wasn't just a cleaner version of a 12-year-old website.

We created a stronger foundation for the product to evolve from, while making the experience clearer, more modern and more focused on the customer.

And the impact was measurable. 1,456 → 2,345 monthly visitors. That's a 61% increase in average monthly traffic after the redesign.

But the biggest win was the shift in thinking. Across my team, our design function and most importantly leadership, we stopped asking: “How do we make the website look better?” and started framing each new feature as way to scale our business and improve team dynamics while building a product that people want to use, return to and ultimately trust when they're looking for their next home.
